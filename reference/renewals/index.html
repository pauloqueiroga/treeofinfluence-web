Example: Why large customers are not renewing

A fictional session from start to finish: 12 leads from 5 functions map why their largest customers are leaving.

Context

This example is fictional. It is the session that the Facilitation pages follow step by step, told here in one place with the whole tree.

A software company sells scheduling and dispatch software to field-service businesses. In one quarter, 3 of its top 20 customers left. Their exit interviews named outages during the morning rush, slow support, and promised features that never shipped.

Each of the 5 functions involved had its own explanation. Engineering pointed at sales commitments, sales at outages, and support at tooling. One of the 2 customer success leads owns renewals. She decided that those explanations mattered more than her own map, and called a session.

Goal

Understand why large customers are not renewing, over the next 2 quarters.

Pricing was named as out of scope before the session. When it came up anyway, it went to the parking lot.

How the tree was built

The session had 12 participants: leads from engineering (4), support (2), product (2), sales (2), and customer success (2). It ran for 2.5 hours on a digital board, with a break after Connect. The customer success lead facilitated it from inside the group.

  1. Prepare. The facilitator pre-seeded the board from the exit interviews, the incident log, and the support queue: 5 problems, 4 outcomes, and 2 actions already under way.
  2. Seed. In 25 minutes, the board grew to about 60 nodes, among them duplicates and solutions.
  3. Group. Five clusters formed: account health, support and escalations, deals and roadmap, releases and reliability, and incident communication. Duplicates were stacked, and 3 nodes went to the parking lot.
  4. Connect. The clusters became a tree. A loop dissolved once its hidden causes were on the board, an obvious arrow was challenged and removed, and an arrow nobody expected joined 2 clusters.
  5. Prioritize. Each participant placed 3 votes on the 14 problems.
  6. Decompose. Three subgroups of 4 each took one of the 3 problems with the most votes into a deep dive.
  7. Close. The group read the tree back, named 2 insights, completed the legend, and agreed when the tree comes back.

For how long each step took, see the example in timing templates.

A tree of this size cannot be read at a glance, and it does not have to be. The full tree is a map. The group worked in views of it.

Of the 47 nodes, 21 passed the filtering. Most are about releases and reliability, as expected. Some are not: "Sales promises custom features to close deals" and "Tier 1 support cannot see a customer's setup" both have a path to outages.

One node at a time

In Decompose, the support subgroup took "Most tickets are escalated to engineering" into a deep-dive sub-tree. The outages node needed more time than the session had, so it got a deep-dive session of its own a week later.

What it revealed

  • Sales promises reached all the way to outages. Custom promises reshuffle the roadmap, so reliability work never makes it into a sprint. No function had drawn that path alone: it crosses 3 of them.
  • Customers are more likely to forgive an outage they hear about from the company first. The group began with an arrow from outages straight to renewals. The facilitator's renewal data did not support it. What mattered was how customers found out, and that became its own part of the tree.
  • A problem with 1 vote sat under a problem with 5. "Tier 1 support cannot see a customer's setup" drew a single vote. Following the arrows showed it under escalations, and escalations under the missing reliability work. See the example in Prioritize.
  • In its deep dive, the support subgroup found that tier 1 is measured on time to close, and escalating is the fastest way to close a ticket.

Lessons

  • Invite every function the scope touches. Each function arrived with its own explanation. The tree showed how they connect, and no single one was the answer.
  • Question the obvious arrows. The arrow everyone agreed on was the one that did not hold. See the challenge pattern.
  • Read the votes with the arrows. Votes show where concern is concentrated. The arrows show what sits underneath it.
  • Work in views. A tree of 47 nodes stays useful when the group reads it one outcome or one node at a time.
  • Leave with an owner and a date. The facilitator owns this tree, with the commitment to raise it again at quarterly planning in 3 months. See a living document.
